Do Vision Encoders Exhibit Human-like Color Thresholds?

Abstract

Understanding and characterizing human color perception is a longstanding research goal. One of the most traditional approaches is looking for human color discrimination thresholds, the minimum chromatic differences perceptible to human observers. Deep vision encoders, foundation models trained on large-scale visual data, map images into latent feature representations; despite their widespread use, few studies have investigated whether these representations exhibit human-like discrimination thresholds.

We present a large-scale exploratory study probing the chromatic sensitivity of more than 50 pretrained vision encoders, including convolutional networks and vision transformers, against human discrimination thresholds. Using controlled chromatic stimuli at multiple chroma levels, we compare model-derived discrimination thresholds with human discrimination ellipses through a region-overlap metric (mIoU). Alignment is generally weak across all model families, with the best mIoU below 0.25. Self-supervised encoders consistently outperform supervised ones, while language-supervised models show the most polarized behavior, occupying both the top and bottom of the ranking. These findings suggest that human-like chromatic sensitivity does not emerge naturally from current large-scale visual training objectives for any of the analyzed architectures.

Key findings

01

Encoders learn color structure, but not human thresholds

Every encoder develops anisotropic similarity fields whose shape changes with hue, mirroring the qualitative non-uniformity of human vision. Their extent and boundaries, however, diverge from the psychophysical ellipses: the best model reaches an mIoU of about 0.22.

02

The training objective matters more than scale

Self-supervised encoders (e.g. DINOv2, EVA-02) generally align best. Classical CNNs such as VGG16 remain competitive with much larger transformer-based foundation models, so more parameters do not translate into more human-like color thresholds.

03

Language supervision is polarizing

AIM-v2 ranks first among all encoders, while several other language-supervised models rank among the lowest. CLIP-style fields are smooth and diffuse, consistent with a semantically compressed space that de-emphasizes fine chromatic distinctions.

04

Blues and saturation are hard

Alignment depends strongly on hue and chroma. Blue regions, also notoriously non-uniform for humans, score low for nearly every encoder, especially at high chroma. The ranking of models, however, stays stable across conditions.

Results

Ranking all encoders by mIoU shows a low ceiling and a narrow spread: no training paradigm approaches near-human agreement.

Why it matters

Frozen embeddings from foundation models are increasingly used for color-sensitive tasks such as color matching, digital art preservation and fine-grained product inspection. If a model places a perceptually distinct color change inside a region of high latent similarity, any downstream task built on that embedding will fail to preserve human-aligned color fidelity, particularly in highly saturated or non-uniform regions of color space.

Future work will move from flat patches to textured surfaces and object renderings, and will compare the orientation and eccentricity of model-induced similarity fields directly with the psychophysical ellipses.
